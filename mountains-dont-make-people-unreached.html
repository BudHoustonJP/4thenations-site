Clarity for the Nations · Data & Frontier Peoples

The Alps did not keep the Reformation out of Tyrol, and rugged terrain does not keep people unreached today.

Innsbruck’s first Protestant church opened in May 1906, nearly four centuries after Wittenberg. Look up from any street and the explanation seems obvious: the mountains kept it out. It is a tidy theory, and it is wrong. The same theory was then tested against today’s people groups. It failed there too, and the reason it failed is the lesson.

Expelled in winter

The mountains did not remove anyone. Rulers did. In the Defereggen Valley the Archbishop of Salzburg expelled Protestants in the middle of winter and kept their children under 15 behind to be raised Catholic. Switch the scale to see the smaller expulsions next to the largest.

Testing it today

How the terrain test was built

History is one thing. Does terrain still separate people from the gospel now? The question went to the Joshua Project dataset. Open each step to see how the test was designed so that mountains, and only mountains, would be measured.

Drawn from the current Joshua Project people group file. Diaspora groups, Deaf communities, and groups listed as scattered were left out, since a single point on a map tells us little about where they live.

A global elevation model measured how rugged the land is within about 130 km of each group’s mapped location.

Groups were compared only with other groups in the same country. That holds laws, persecution, colonial history, and national church strength constant.

A Nepali group is measured against other Nepali groups, not against Brazil. If a mountainous country also has harsh laws, a global comparison would blame the mountains for what the laws did. A within-country comparison cannot make that mistake.

A single point can mislead for a large group. So every Joshua Project profile for groups of 1 million or more was read and coded for where the group lives. Make the call

If mountains still keep people unreached, what should the data show?

The test made three predictions. If terrain still separates people from the gospel, the most rugged groups in each country should come out worse on all three. Make your call on each, then reveal the result.

The idea failed.

Tyrol already pointed the same way. The gospel arrived within three years, by road and by mine. Rulers, not rocks, decided what could stay in the open. And the mountains did something unexpected: they hid the believers, protecting whatever people already held.

The full result, and what it means for how we think about unreached peoples today, is in the essay.
