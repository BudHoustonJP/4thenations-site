Rising income buys a national church three things, and the offering is the weakest of them.

Credentialing clears a border no religious worker visa ever will. Mobility turns moving abroad into a career decision instead of a family crisis. Only after those two does a bigger offering show up. Korea proved this runs on a schedule. Here is where the schedule is running now.

1963: the same starting line

Korea and Ghana began at a hundred dollars a year

In 1963, a factory worker in Seoul earned roughly what a cocoa farmer outside Accra earned: about a hundred dollars a year. Then one curve took off and the other did not. Drag the year and watch the gap open, and watch when the missionaries appear.

Two kinds of evidence

Sort the curve from the noise

A rising GDP headline and a squeezed household budget can both be true at the same time. A structural signal moves over a decade and rarely reverses fast. A cyclical signal moves over a year or two and reverses often. Tap each signal to classify it. Tap again to change your answer.

Order of magnitude

Three growth rates, three different things being measured

The essay names three growth figures. They are not the same measure, which is exactly why they should be read as an order of magnitude rather than a ranking. What they share is a slope steep enough to change who a church can send.

Three different measures shown at one scale, for order of magnitude only. Korea: GDP per capita growth, roughly 9.6 percent annually, 1970 to 1991. Ethiopia: IMF projected growth for 2026, 9.2 percent. India: projected middle class growth, roughly 8.5 percent annually through 2030. Sources per the essay: comparative Korea and Ghana economic studies, IMF World Economic Outlook April 2026, and World Data Lab via World Population Review.
